FREE IELTS SPEAKING LESSON · SEPTEMBER 10, 2026

Turn one minute of notes into a two-minute answer

Build a four-part note map for an original Speaking Part 2 cue card, record one uninterrupted answer and diagnose the moments where your ideas stopped connecting.

What the long turn requires

The official IELTS Speaking format describes Part 2 as an individual long turn. You receive a task card, have one minute to prepare with pencil and paper, and should be ready to keep speaking for two minutes. The examiner may ask one or two related questions afterward. The complete Part 2 section, including preparation, lasts about three to four minutes.

Speaking is assessed throughout the test using four criteria: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. A useful plan therefore provides an order for ideas and reminders of specific details. It should not become a script that you try to memorise word for word.

ORIGINAL PRACTICE CARD

Describe a place where you can concentrate well.

You should say:

  • where the place is
  • when you usually go there
  • what you do there
  • and explain why it helps you concentrate.

Use four boxes instead of full sentences

Place

quiet library
second floor
window desk

Routine

Saturday morning
after breakfast
two hours

Concrete detail

phone in bag
paper checklist
soft page sounds

Meaning

clear boundary
less temptation
leave with progress

Each box contains prompts rather than sentences. The place and routine answer the first two bullet points. Concrete details give you material to describe. Meaning explains the final bullet point and gives the response a reason to continue beyond a list.

A sixty-second preparation routine

  1. 0–10 seconds: choose one real or believable place. Do not compare several options.
  2. 10–25 seconds: note where it is and one regular time you visit.
  3. 25–40 seconds: add two visible actions and one sensory detail.
  4. 40–52 seconds: write a cause-and-effect explanation for why concentration improves.
  5. 52–60 seconds: decide your opening and final reflection. Check that every cue-card point has a prompt.

If you cannot remember a precise word, write a simpler alternative during preparation. The official description of lexical resource includes expressing meaning with alternative vocabulary when a particular word is unavailable. Keeping the idea moving is more useful than waiting silently for a perfect term.

Shape the answer as a short story

Begin by naming the place and its role in your week. Move into a typical visit, describe what you physically do, then explain the effect on your attention. Finish with a reflection or small contrast. This sequence creates progression without forcing artificial linking phrases into every sentence.

  • Opening: identify the place and give the listener a reason it matters.
  • Scene: locate the desk, time and atmosphere.
  • Action: explain the study routine with one specific example.
  • Reflection: connect the environment to concentration and a result.

Original sample response

The place where I concentrate best is the public library near my apartment. I usually go there on Saturday mornings, when the second floor is quiet and I can sit at a desk beside a large window. It has become a small weekly routine for me.

I normally arrive just after breakfast and stay for about two hours. Before I start, I put my phone inside my bag rather than leaving it on the desk. Then I write three tasks on paper. Last weekend, for example, I used the first hour to revise a presentation and the second to read an article I had postponed during the week.

The library helps because it creates a clear boundary between study time and everything else. At home, I notice unfinished chores and I can easily open another browser tab. In the library, other people are reading quietly, so working feels like the natural thing to do. I can still hear pages turning and chairs moving, but those soft sounds are less distracting than complete silence.

What I like most is leaving with visible progress. Even if I only finish two items on my list, I know I used the morning deliberately. That feeling makes the library more than a quiet room; it is a place that changes the way I behave.

The response follows the note map but does not recite the cue-card bullets in order. It develops one example, explains causes and finishes with meaning. Use it to observe structure, not as a script to memorise.

Record and review one attempt

  1. Set a one-minute preparation timer and write only keywords.
  2. Start a separate two-minute timer and record one uninterrupted answer.
  3. Listen once for ideas: mark any point where the listener would not know how one sentence relates to the next.
  4. Listen again for language: note repeated words, unfinished grammar and words that were difficult to pronounce clearly.
  5. Choose one improvement and record the same card again. Do not rewrite the entire answer.

Do not judge success only by reaching two minutes. A longer answer can still repeat itself. Check whether each section adds a place, action, detail, cause or reflection.

Self-review checklist

  • Did I address all four cue-card prompts without reading them aloud as a list?
  • Could a listener follow a beginning, a typical visit and a final explanation?
  • Did I develop at least one specific example?
  • When I forgot a word, did I explain it another way and continue?
  • Were key words understandable without forcing an unfamiliar accent?
  • Can I identify one pause caused by missing ideas and one caused by missing language?

Repeat with three variations

Keep the same four-box plan and change the subject: a café where you enjoy meeting someone, an outdoor place where you relax, or a room where you learned an important skill. A repeatable structure should support different content. If every answer uses the same memorised sentences, simplify your notes and speak from the new details.
