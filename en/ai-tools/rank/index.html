How this index is built

No mystique, just the criteria.

1. Why rank by growth instead of traffic

Sort by traffic and the top twenty is always the same: ChatGPT, Canva, Midjourney. That ranking is useless to anyone who builds sites, because nobody is going to build another ChatGPT.

The useful signal is the site that nobody had heard of last month and that tripled its traffic this month. What did those sites do in common? Which acquisition channel did they use? Which narrow need did they carve out? That is what can actually be learned. So this index has exactly one sorting rule: month-over-month growth, descending.

2. Where the numbers come from

Domains, descriptions, categories, monthly visits and growth figures come from public third-party traffic estimates and publicly available site information, then each entry is checked by hand to confirm the domain resolves and the description matches what the tool actually does.

  • Monthly visits is a third-party estimate rounded to K or M. It is not exact. The same site can differ by 20 to 50 percent between providers, so read it as an order of magnitude, not a measurement.
  • Growth is the month-over-month percentage change in visits. Sites starting from a very small base — 30 visits becoming 3,000 — produce absurd percentages, so everything above a threshold is capped at +9900%.
  • Registration date, organic search share and domain rating are reserved fields. Where they have not been filled in per site, they are left blank rather than guessed.

One thing has to be said plainly: these are estimates, not audited data. They are good for reading trend and magnitude, and unsuitable for any financial decision.

3. Why only nineteen sites

Because every entry is verified by hand, and a short honest list beats a long padded one. An entry has to satisfy all three: the domain resolves, the product does one clearly defined thing rather than everything, and it grew noticeably in the past month. The list will expand gradually; only at a few hundred entries does an index like this start to be statistically meaningful.

If an entry has gone offline or the description is wrong, tell us through the contact link in the footer. Correcting data matters more than adding it.

What this batch of data suggests

Nineteen sites is a small sample, but a few patterns are already visible.

1. The fastest risers do exactly one narrow thing

Look at the top of the list: turn a YouTube video into a transcript, generate a product mascot, turn a selfie into a job-application headshot, detect whether an article was written by AI. Each of those fits in one sentence, which means a visitor can decide within seconds whether it is what they came for.

The sites that describe themselves as an all-in-one AI creation platform consistently rank lower on growth. The reason is not subtle: all-in-one means the homepage has to explain ten things, and a visitor who cannot find the point in three seconds leaves. A single-purpose tool puts the answer in the first screen, which is the shortest possible conversion path.

2. Categories are heavily concentrated

Image generation and text generation dominate this sample. The image share is highest, and that tracks directly with the collapse in generation costs over the past year: once the unit cost of an image or a short video dropped, small free-traffic tool sites became economically viable.

3. Language localisation is an underrated lever

Two entries deserve a closer look: an AI-detection tool aimed at Korean users, and a multimodal studio for Russian speakers built around the promise of working without a VPN. Their absolute traffic is modest — tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand visits a month — but each of them rebuilt an existing capability for a language region the English giants do not serve well.

That path suits small teams: the engineering is not hard, competition is thin, and users in those markets are often more willing to pay for a native interface. The only real barrier is that you have to genuinely understand how people write in that language. A machine-translated interface does not fool anyone.

4. High growth is not the same as a good business

A bucket of cold water is necessary here. Growth means people are arriving; it does not mean anyone is paying. A large share of this sample are free tools monetised by ads, living off long-tail search traffic — traffic climbing quickly while revenue per user sits near zero. Whether an AI tool site survives depends on retention and paid conversion, and neither of those is available through public data.

So the correct way to use this index is as a signal source for which needs are currently being validated, not as a list of sites worth copying.
