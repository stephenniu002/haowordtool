From brief to delivery, in three steps.

01 · Define the brief

Use the checklist in each guide to define the goal, assets, budget and deadline. A written brief protects both sides: the freelancer quotes accurately instead of padding for uncertainty, and you have something concrete to compare when two quotes differ wildly.

02 · Compare like for like

Compare portfolios, files, revisions and usage terms. Request a quote for the full scope — not the cheapest package on the page. Two quotes are only comparable when they cover the same deliverables, the same number of revision rounds, and the same usage rights.

03 · Review the result

Check the agreed files and functions. Handle payment and disputes through the platform used. Accept the delivery only when it matches the written brief — checking now is far cheaper than discovering a missing source file three months later.

How to get a good quote from any freelancer

Good quotes start with good questions. Before you accept a price, confirm exactly what is included: the list of deliverables, the file formats you will receive, how many revision rounds are covered, the delivery date, and what usage rights transfer to you. A quote that answers all five is worth more than a cheaper one that answers two.

Describe your scope in writing and ask the freelancer to confirm it back in their own words. This single habit eliminates most misunderstandings — if their summary does not match your brief, you have caught the problem before any money changed hands. For anything beyond a small fixed task, ask for the quote broken down by milestone so you can review progress along the way.

Finally, compare the total cost of ownership, not the headline price. A logo for $50 with no source files and no usage rights can cost more than a $200 package that includes everything, once you factor in the redesign you will need later. The cheapest quote is only cheap if it covers what you actually need.

How we maintain these guides

Each guide carries a last-updated date and is reviewed on a regular cycle. We rewrite sections — not just the date — when something material changes: a platform updates its pricing model or terms, a new category of service becomes common, or reader feedback shows a section is confusing or out of date.

What does not trigger a rewrite is fashion. We do not reshuffle recommendations to chase trends, and we do not add providers because they asked us to. If a guide's advice still holds, it stays as it is; the updated date tells you we checked.
