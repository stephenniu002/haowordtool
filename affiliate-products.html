How we choose products: the framework behind our buying guides

This page used to list a hundred products with a few templated lines under each one. We deleted that list, because it helped nobody: a hundred shallow entries cannot tell you whether any single product deserves your money, and repeating the same phrases under different product names is not research. What replaced it is the framework our guides actually use — the five checks we run before writing about a product category, and the category notes for the areas this site covers in depth. If you are choosing between products right now, run the same five checks yourself; they take ten minutes and they catch most bad purchases before checkout.

Check 1: Name the job the product has to do

Every disappointing purchase starts as a vague wish. "Something warm for the sofa" becomes a good decision only when it turns into a job statement: warm my shoulders for two evening hours, from the power bank I already own, without a wall socket nearby. Write your job statement in one sentence before you open a single listing. It immediately deletes most of the market — products too weak, too bulky, wrong-powered or wrong-sized for that job — and it converts marketing adjectives into yes-or-no questions. If a product page cannot answer your job statement from its specifications, that silence is itself an answer.

Check 2: Compare the dimensions that decide the category

Every category has two or three specifications that determine whether owners are happy, surrounded by twenty decorative ones. Heated wearables live or die on power delivery and heating-zone placement. Smart glasses stand or fall on whether their headline features work through your phone or on the device, and on all-day weight. Outfits succeed on fabric composition and cut, not on how they photograph. Our category notes below list the deciding dimensions for the areas we cover. When you compare two products, compare those dimensions first, side by side, from the manufacturers' own specification tables — not from the bullet points a marketplace seller chose to enlarge.

Check 3: Count the total cost, not the price

The sticker is the smallest part of many purchases. Add the consumables and companions the product quietly requires: the power bank with enough capacity to matter, the replacement filters, the subscription that unlocks the advertised feature, the case that should have been in the box. Then divide realistically by lifespan: a product used two hundred evenings costs a fraction per evening of one abandoned after three, which is why Check 1 outranks every discount. Our price-breakdown guides work through this arithmetic category by category instead of announcing winners.

Check 4: Read reviews for evidence, not averages

A 4.6 average built on two thousand ratings tells you less than twenty detailed reviews with photographs. Read the three-star reviews first — they are usually written by people who liked the idea and can name the specific thing that fell short. Sort by most recent, because products change silently mid-listing. Treat any review that reads like the product description as decoration. And treat our own guides the same way: we do not publish star ratings or test scores we cannot document, so where a guide expresses a preference it shows the reasoning, and you can weigh it yourself.

Check 5: Price the risk of being wrong

Some purchases are cheap to reverse and some are not. Before buying, check the return window, who pays return shipping, and whether the warranty is honoured by the manufacturer or only by a marketplace storefront that may not exist next season. For first-generation categories — AI wearables are the current example — being wrong is more likely, so the return terms matter more, and buying from a channel with a real returns process beats saving a small amount through one without. If being wrong would sting, wait a week; a job statement that survives a week is a real need, and one that evaporates was a mood.

Category note: heated shawls and USB heated wearables

The deciding dimensions are power and placement. Check the required input (a shawl that wants more current than a standard USB port supplies will feel lukewarm — the most common complaint in the category, and usually an electrical mismatch rather than a defect), where the heating elements sit relative to your job statement, and the honest runtime arithmetic for your power bank. Our four-part series works through each: the buying guide, the power bank runtime calculations, the price-tier breakdown and the USB power troubleshooting guide. Skip the category entirely if your job needs outdoor-grade heat in wind — these are indoor, close-to-body warmers, and no specification on the box changes that.

Category note: AI smart glasses

The deciding dimensions are dependency and wearability. Establish which features run on the glasses and which are your phone doing the work through an app, because that determines battery life, latency and what still functions when your phone is in a bag. Weigh the frame — a few grams decide whether a translation or camera feature gets used daily or monthly. Then apply Check 5 strictly: this is a young category with fast model turnover, so buy on return terms, not on launch promises. Our AI glasses page presents the model this site sells with its specifications stated plainly; hold it to the same five checks as everything else here.

Category note: everyday outfits

Fashion fails Check 2 more than any other category, because photographs are engineered to hide the deciding dimensions: fabric composition (read the percentage label, not the adjectives), construction at the seams, and how a cut behaves on a body that moves rather than poses. Our outfits page collects the looks this site publishes, each tied to the kind of day it is actually for. Buy the fabric and the fit you can verify; let the styling be the bonus, never the reason.
