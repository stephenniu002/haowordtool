How these boards are built

Every board on this page follows the same admission rule: a product gets listed only if there is public evidence that it earns money and the model behind it is small enough for a solo builder or a tiny team to copy. A famous product with a two-hundred-person team behind it teaches you nothing you can use this month, so it does not appear here no matter how impressive the revenue. A two-person tool with a public pricing page, visible customers and a growth channel you can inspect teaches a great deal, so it qualifies even at modest numbers. Each entry answers two questions in plain language — what the product does, and which specific part of its playbook is worth copying — because a board that only ranks without explaining is a billboard, not a research tool.

Read the revenue numbers the right way

Income figures on these boards are reported numbers: they come from founders' own posts, public revenue dashboards and interviews, and nobody independent audits them. Treat every figure as the top of a range, not a fact. Our standing advice is to divide a self-reported number by five before you let it influence a decision, and to pay more attention to the shape of the business than to its headline: what exactly is sold, whether payment is one-off or recurring, whether the price survives a refund rate, and whether the founder shows a second and third month rather than one launch spike. A product honestly earning a steady four figures a month from subscriptions is a better teacher than a screenshot of a single five-figure launch day.

How to use a board without fooling yourself

Start from a category you already understand — a tool for a job you have done, a content format you already publish, a community you already belong to. Copying works when the copier brings one unfair advantage: distribution, domain knowledge or an existing audience. Pick one entry and write down its three moving parts: the offer, the price point and the channel that brings buyers. Then ask which of the three you can reproduce with your own assets and which one was luck, timing or a platform loophole that has since closed. If two of the three depend on conditions that no longer exist, admire the entry and move on. The boards are updated as entries launch, pivot or quietly die, and dead entries are marked rather than silently deleted, because a board that hides its failures will eventually mislead you about the base rate of success.

A worked example of reading an entry

Say a board entry describes a small AI tool whose founder reports a few thousand dollars a month from a subscription priced for individual professionals. The copyable parts are concrete: a narrow job, a price a single user can expense without asking a manager, and a channel — short demonstration videos — that a second builder could also operate. The non-copyable part is usually time: the founder started before the category had a name, and search results still reward that head start. Reading the entry this way turns it from inspiration into a checklist — offer, price, channel, head start — and tells you honestly which line of the four you would have to replace with something of your own. That is the difference between a board used as research and a board used as entertainment.

What the boards deliberately leave out

There are no traffic estimates scraped from third-party panels, no invented conversion rates and no paid placements. Products cannot buy a position, and affiliate relationships, where they exist, are disclosed on the page that carries them. The boards also skip entire categories — crypto speculation, dropshipping arbitrage and anything whose revenue depends on deceiving a platform — not because they never make money, but because they do not compound into a durable business a reader can build on. What remains is narrower and, we think, more useful: a map of small, real, copyable ways people are currently earning with AI.
