How to Write AI Animation Scripts with ChatGPT: The 5-Step Prompt Chain

Turn an idea into a shootable 30-second animation script in 20 minutes. The exact 5-step ChatGPT prompt chain: premise, beat sheet, dialogue pass, visual pass, read-aloud test.

By HaoWord Studio Editorial · 6 min · Updated 2026-09-25

You have a beat sheet — hook, beats, payoff, timed to 30 seconds. Now you need a real script: dialogue with distinct character voices, action lines an animator (or an AI image generator) can actually use, and timing that survives contact with a stopwatch.

With the right prompt chain, a large language model like ChatGPT or Claude takes you from beat sheet to shootable draft in about twenty minutes. The key word is chain: five short, focused prompts in sequence, each building on the last. One giant "write me a script" prompt produces mush. Five precise prompts produce a production document.

Here is the exact chain.

Step 1: The Premise Prompt — Generate Options, Then Choose

Start wide. Give the model your format constraints and ask for multiple premises:

"I'm making a 30-second AI-animated comedy short. Main character: a nervous robot chef who takes everything literally. Give me 3 premise ideas, each with a one-line payoff. Keep each premise under 40 words."

Why three options? Because your first idea is rarely your best, and comparing options activates your taste — which is the one thing the AI cannot supply. Pick one premise and commit. Decision made, move on.

Step 2: Feed It Your Beat Sheet — Structure In, Script Out

Now give the model your structure and ask it to expand:

"Here's my structure. Hook: ___. Beat 1: ___. Beat 2: ___. Beat 3: ___. Payoff: ___. Expand this into a script with dialogue and action lines, timed to 30 seconds total. Format each beat with its timestamp."

This is where the beat sheet pays off. Because you already solved the story architecture, the model is doing pure execution — filling in dialogue and blocking within a frame you designed. The output will need editing, but the skeleton will be sound.

Step 3: The Dialogue Pass — Give Each Character a Voice

First drafts from AI tend to make every character sound like the same polite narrator. Fix it with a dedicated pass:

"Now rewrite the dialogue so each character has a distinct voice. The robot speaks in short, literal sentences. The customer speaks fast and dramatic. Keep total dialogue under 75 words."

The 75-word cap is doing real work here: thirty seconds of screen time cannot hold more, and the constraint forces the model to cut filler instead of you doing it later. Distinct voices matter enormously in animation, where the audience cannot see an actor's face doing half the work.

Step 4: The Visual Pass — Write What the Camera Sees

Animation scripts need a visual layer that stage plays do not. Add it explicitly:

"Add a VISUAL line under each dialogue line describing what we see, in simple animation-friendly terms: character position, expression, and camera angle. Keep each visual line under 15 words."

"Simple animation-friendly terms" is the critical phrase. You want "medium shot, robot holds up burnt cake, proud expression" — not "in a breathtaking tour de force of culinary despair." Every visual line you write here becomes a prompt ingredient for your image generator later, so clarity now saves regeneration cycles later.

Step 5: The Read-Aloud Test — Cut 20 Percent

Print the script or pull it up on your phone. Read the entire thing out loud — voices, pauses, everything — with a timer running. AI almost always overwrites, and the read-aloud is where the bloat becomes undeniable.

For example, hypothetically: imagine a draft where the robot explains the joke for ten full seconds before the visual punchline. In this illustration, cutting that explanation entirely makes the short funnier — the burnt cake on screen is the joke, and the dialogue was just narrating what the audience can already see. Animation shows; dialogue should tell as little as possible.

Your target: the read-aloud lands at or under 30 seconds with natural pacing. If it runs long, cut whole lines before trimming words — a removed beat beats a rushed delivery.

The Mindset: AI Is Your Junior Writer, Not Your Showrunner

This chain works because of a clear division of labor. The AI drafts fast, generates options, and handles formatting. You make the taste calls: which premise survives, which joke stays, which line gets cut, whether the payoff actually earns its twist.

Beginners who get poor results from AI scripting usually abdicate the showrunner role — they accept the first draft wholesale and blame the tool. The five minutes you spend choosing, cutting, and rewriting are the five minutes that make the script yours.

One more habit that compounds: save every prompt that works in a note called your "prompt library." The chain above, tuned to your genre and characters, will take five minutes instead of twenty on your next short — and you will reuse it for every short after that.

Summary

Turn a beat sheet into a shootable 30-second script with five chained prompts: (1) generate three premise options and pick one, (2) expand your beat sheet into a timed draft, (3) rewrite dialogue for distinct character voices under 75 words, (4) add concise visual lines for camera and action, (5) read aloud with a timer and cut 20 percent. You are the showrunner; the AI is the junior writer.

FAQ

Can ChatGPT really write animation scripts?

It can draft them well when given structure. The winning formula is: you supply the beat sheet (hook, beats, payoff, timings) and the AI expands it into dialogue and action lines. AI-first, structure-second produces generic scripts; structure-first, AI-second produces usable ones.

How long should dialogue be in a 30-second animated short?

Under 75 words total. That is roughly 25–28 seconds of spoken dialogue at a natural pace, leaving room for pauses, reactions, and the visual payoff to breathe.

Should dialogue describe what is happening on screen?

No — that is the most common beginner error. If the audience can see the burnt cake, the character should not announce "Look, a burnt cake." Animation shows; dialogue should add what visuals cannot: personality, subtext, and timing.

How do I keep AI from making all characters sound the same?

Use a dedicated dialogue pass with explicit voice rules per character ("short literal sentences" vs. "fast and dramatic"), and cap the word count. Then read the lines aloud yourself — if you cannot tell who is speaking without the labels, the voices need another pass.
