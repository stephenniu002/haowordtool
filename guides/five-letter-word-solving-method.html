A Better Method for Five-Letter Word Puzzles

Use position information, letter frequency, and contradiction tracking instead of chasing fashionable starter words.

By HaoWord Studio Editorial · 18 min · Updated 2026-10-30

Separate three kinds of evidence

Treat confirmed positions, present-but-misplaced letters, and excluded letters as different data. If A is green in position two, lock it there. If R is yellow, keep R but ban its tested position. Mixing these facts mentally causes repeated guesses that teach you nothing.

Choose information-rich guesses

An early guess should test common letters without unnecessary duplication. SLATE samples five frequent letters; MUMMY repeats M and offers much less information unless duplicates are already suspected. The best second guess depends on the first result, so no universal starter word is automatically optimal.

Handle repeated letters carefully

A gray letter can still occur when the guess used that letter more times than the answer. If LEVEL produces one confirmed E and one gray E, the answer may contain exactly one E. Record letter counts, not only presence. This is the same multiset logic used by a reliable unscrambler.

Finish with constraints, not intuition

Before submitting, read the pattern aloud as a template: _ A _ E R, contains T, excludes S/L/N. Generate only words that satisfy every condition, then choose the candidate that either solves or separates the remaining possibilities. A disciplined final check prevents attractive but impossible guesses.

A real game, step by step

Method only sticks when you can see it work. Here is a full game played with the rules above — answer hidden, each step written exactly as you would do it. (This trace uses the answer CRANE; cover the rest of the paragraph on your first read-through and try to beat it.)

  1. Guess 1 — gather information, don't aim to win. Play SLATE. Result: S gray, L gray, A green at position 3, T gray, E green at position 5. Template: _ _ A _ E. Excluded: S, L, T. This is a good first guess: three letters eliminated, two locked, and nothing wasted on a duplicate.
  2. Write the template aloud before touching the keyboard. "Blank-blank-A-blank-E, contains nothing confirmed yet, excludes S, L, T." Saying it out loud is not theater — it stops the common failure of guessing a word that contains an excluded letter because you "forgot".
  3. Guess 2 — test the remaining common letters. The unused frequent letters are R, O, I, N, C, D, P. Play BRAVE: B gray, R yellow (not position 2), A green, V gray, E green. Template updates to: _ R A _ E, excludes S, L, T, B, V. R must sit in position 1 or 4.
  4. Generate only from the template. Words fitting _ R A _ E: CRANE, CRAKE, GRAPE, DRAKE, FRAME, GRACE. Every one satisfies green A-3, green E-5, R present but not at position 2, no excluded letters. If your candidate list contains anything else, your template is wrong — go back to step 2.
  5. Play the guess that separates, not the one you like. CRANE, CRAKE, and GRACE share C-R-A-_ -E shapes. Guess CRANE: if C is gray at position 1, the answer is GRACE or DRAKE; if C is green, it's CRANE or CRAKE. One guess, three outcomes — that's a separating guess.
  6. Submit, then log. In this trace CRANE solves on guess 3. Write one line in your log: "SLATE → __A_E, BRAVE → _RA_E, CRANE solved; R-yellow handled." The log is what turns a lucky game into a repeatable skill.

Notice what never happened: at no point did anyone "feel" the answer. Every guess was dictated by the template and the remaining letter pool.

Starter words, compared honestly

Count the ten most common letters in five-letter answer lists — roughly E, A, R, I, O, T, N, S, L, C — and score starters by how many of them they test without duplication. The numbers below are derived from that letter list, so you can check the arithmetic yourself.

The honest conclusion: SLATE, CRANE, and RAISE are interchangeable in practice. What separates players is not the starter — it's whether guess 2 responds to guess 1. A fixed second word played blind is worth less than an average first word plus a thoughtful second.

A pitfall we hit ourselves: the gray-E trap

While testing this method we ran a game that broke it in our heads. Answer: STEEL. Guess: LEVEL. Result: one E confirmed, one E gray. Our next guess excluded E entirely — "E is gray, so no E" — and we spent three guesses circling a word that contained an E we had already seen confirmed.

The rule we wrote down afterward: gray means "no more of this letter than the confirmed count", not "none of this letter". One green E plus one gray E means exactly one E. Record it as a count (E: 1), not as a ban. The same multiset logic applies in the unscrambler: a duplicate in your guess does not create duplicates in the answer.

Second trap from the same testing session: after a yellow R at position 2, we kept "remembering" that R was excluded — it wasn't, only position 2 was banned — and nearly threw away the winning guess. Now our template always writes yellows as "R: not-2" rather than striking the letter. Small notation, big difference.

FAQ

Should I change my starter word every day?
No. A fixed starter turns guess 1 into a calibrated instrument: you learn exactly how much information "SLATE → __A_E" gives you, and your guess-2 patterns get sharper. Rotate starters only when you're deliberately practicing adaptability, not when you're trying to win.
What does a gray letter really mean?
It means the answer contains no more of that letter than the number already confirmed green or yellow. A gray E alongside a green E means exactly one E. A gray letter with no confirmed copies means zero. Write counts, not bans.
How do I handle answers with double letters?
Suspect them when your template has two or three empty slots but few unused common letters left. Test with a guess that repeats the suspected letter (STEEL-style) rather than introducing new letters — one guess confirms or kills the duplicate theory.
Is hard mode strategy different?
Yes, and it's stricter: every guess must reuse confirmed information, so you can't spend a guess purely probing new letters. The template discipline matters more here — in hard mode, a sloppy guess 2 doesn't just waste a turn, it can trap you with no safe moves left.
My second guess always feels like a shot in the dark. How do I fix that?
List the unused frequent letters after guess 1 (R, O, I, N, C, D, P, H) and pick a guess covering the most of them while respecting your template. In the worked game above, BRAVE tested R and the vowels' leftovers while honoring __A_E. "Feels random" usually means you skipped writing the template.
Does letter frequency differ in non-English versions?
Yes — the top-10 list above is for English answer lists. Other languages have different letter distributions, so the starter table doesn't transfer directly. The method does: separate the three kinds of evidence, test distinct letters early, and track counts for repeats.

Quick review

The strongest habit is to separate hard constraints—letter counts, length, fixed positions—from helpful clues such as frequency and familiar patterns. Use the tool to narrow possibilities, then use meaning and context to choose.
