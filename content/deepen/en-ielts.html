How to use these lessons

The weekly cycle, adapted to the time that does not include the timer

A practice session has three parts, and most people skip the two that are not timed. Reading the question, planning, and checking the answer take longer than the task itself, which is why a single 40-minute block is worth less than three 15-minute blocks spread across the week.

What each lesson actually trains

Academic Writing Task 2 — the eight-minute plan
The lesson does not ask you to write a full essay. It asks you to produce a plan: identify the instruction word (discuss, evaluate, to what extent), take one position, assign each body paragraph a single job, and develop one idea with a cause-and-effect chain instead of three ideas with no development. Writing happens after the plan, and only after. An essay that is well developed in two paragraphs scores better than one that touches four points and develops none — which is the most common way a strong writer loses marks.
Speaking Part 2 — the note map
One minute of preparation, two minutes of speaking. The note map is four lines, not a script: what happened, where and when, one detail that makes it specific, and what you thought afterwards. The reflection line matters most, because candidates who only narrate run out of content at about 75 seconds and then repeat themselves.

How to check yourself without a teacher

Recording yourself and reading the transcript is more useful than listening for "sounding fluent". On the transcript, look for three things: sentences that start the same way every time, tense shifts inside a single story, and vocabulary that repeats within two lines. Those are countable. "Did I sound good" is not.

For writing, count the words you spent on each paragraph. A 250-word essay with a 110-word introduction and a 40-word conclusion has a body problem, and no amount of polishing the introduction fixes it.

What these lessons are not

These are original practice materials written to explain a process. They do not reproduce official test content, they do not predict a band score, and nothing here replaces the official format description published by the test owners. For the authoritative rules on timing, scoring criteria and what is permitted in the exam room, use the official source for the skill you are practising. Bands are awarded by trained examiners against published criteria; a practice exercise can improve how you organise an answer, not tell you what it will score.

One task per session. Pick a single skill, set the timer, finish, then spend twice as long reviewing as you spent writing. The review is the practice; the timed attempt is only the material for it.
