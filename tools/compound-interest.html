Worked example

Starting with 10,000 and adding 500 a month at 7% for 20 years ends near 264,000. You put in 130,000 of that; the rest is compounding.

Compounding is growth on growth. The first years look unremarkable — the balance barely moves away from what you contribute — and the last years do most of the work. This shape is why starting early beats contributing more later.

What the projection ignores

Inflation, taxes on gains, fees, and the fact that returns are not a constant 7% — they are volatile, and sequence matters. A single bad decade early cannot be averaged away by a good one late. Treat the output as a shape, not a forecast.

Frequency, timing and fees

The compounding frequency in the label is annual, while contributions are monthly, so the monthly deposit is credited as it arrives and the balance is compounded at the stated annual rate. More frequent compounding raises the effective rate: 5 percent compounded monthly is about 5.12 percent a year, and the gap widens with the rate.

Contribution timing matters more than most people expect — money added at the start of the month earns for the whole month, money added at the end does not. Fees compound too, in the opposite direction: one percentage point of annual cost can remove a fifth of the final balance over several decades. For anything measured in today's money, subtract inflation before comparing scenarios.
