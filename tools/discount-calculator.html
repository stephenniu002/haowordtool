Worked example

30% off 120 gives 84. Stacking a further 10% takes it to 75.60, which is 37% off in total — not 40%. Discounts multiply, they do not add.

Stacked discounts are the classic arithmetic trap. "30% off, then an extra 10%" is not 40% off; it is 37%. The second discount applies to the already-reduced price, so the bases differ.

Recovering the original price

If something costs 84 after 30% off, the original is 84 ÷ 0.7 = 120, not 84 × 1.3 = 109.20. Multiplying by 1.3 adds 30% of the sale price, which is a smaller number than 30% of the original.

Tax, rounding and the order of operations

Apply the discount before tax, never after. A 20% discount on a taxed price refunds tax on the discounted portion too, which overstates the saving and understates the tax owed. In most jurisdictions the invoice shows the reduction on the net amount, then tax on what remains.

Rounding is where till receipts and calculations disagree by a cent. Round each line or only the total, and the two routes diverge as soon as you have more than one item; stores pick one and apply it consistently. When reconciling an invoice, compare line by line rather than expecting the totals to match to the last digit.

Fixed-amount discounts behave very differently from percentages. Twenty off a hundred is a fifth; twenty off forty is half. When comparing two offers, convert both to a percentage of the same base before deciding, and check whether the cheaper headline number applies to the item you are actually buying rather than to a bundle that includes something you did not want.
