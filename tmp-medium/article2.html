I used to treat every Short like a brand-new production: new idea, new filming session, new edit. It was exhausting, and my posting schedule was "whenever I felt like it," which is to say, rarely.

Then I flipped the process. Now I record once — one long session, 30 to 60 minutes — and that single recording becomes a full week of Shorts. Here's the exact workflow.

Step 1: Record one long session, badly on purpose

I sit down and talk through a topic for 30–60 minutes. No script, just bullet points. I ramble, I repeat myself, I go off on tangents. This is deliberate: a long, messy recording contains 5–10 usable moments, and I'd never find all of them if I tried to film each one separately.

The only rule: say the hook out loud. If a thought starts with something interesting — a surprising number, a strong opinion, a "here's what nobody tells you" — flag it mentally and keep going.

Gear doesn't matter here. Phone on a tripod, decent light, quiet room. The recording is raw material, not the product.

Step 2: Mine it for moments

I watch (or skim) the recording at 1.5x speed and pull out every 20–60 second segment that works on its own: a complete thought, a tip, a story, a reaction. I aim for 5–10 clips from one session.

What makes a good clip:

  • It starts mid-energy. No throat-clearing, no "so today I want to talk about." The first two seconds decide everything.
  • One idea only. If the clip needs context from the rest of the recording, it's not a clip — it's a fragment.
  • It ends cleanly. A punchline, a takeaway, a question. Viewers should feel the clip is complete even though the topic isn't.

I write down timestamps in a notes app. Nothing fancy.

Step 3: Edit for the feed, not for yourself

This is where most people lose the plot. A repurposed clip isn't finished when it's cut — it needs to be rebuilt for vertical, sound-off, three-second-attention-span viewing:

  1. Reframe to 9:16 with the speaker's face in the upper two-thirds.
  2. Burn in word-by-word captions. Most people watch muted; captions aren't optional.
  3. Cut every silence and filler word. The pace of a Short should feel slightly too fast on first watch.
  4. Add a title hook as on-screen text in the first frame — the thing someone reads before they decide to keep watching.

This step is mechanical, and it's the one I'd outsource first. Step 4: Title, thumbnail frame, and schedule

Each Short gets:

  • A title with a searchable keyword, not a clever one. "How I edit Shorts in 10 minutes" beats "My secret workflow revealed."
  • A thumbnail frame pulled from the most expressive moment — mid-sentence, not the posed start.
  • A posting slot. I schedule the week's clips across 5–7 days instead of dumping them at once. Consistency beats bursts.

Step 5: Read the numbers, feed them back

After a week, I look at which clips held viewers past the 3-second mark and which died. The winners tell me what to talk about in the next long recording. The losers tell me what to stop saying.

This is the part people skip, and it's the whole point of the system: every week of Shorts makes the next recording better. After a month, you're not guessing what works anymore — you have data.

Three mistakes that kill the system

Mistake 1: Recording "content" instead of talking. The moment you perform for the camera, the long session gets stiff and the clips feel scripted. Talk like you're explaining it to a friend. The best-performing clip I ever posted came from a tangent I almost edited out.

Mistake 2: Posting clips in the order you recorded them. Your week should feel varied, not like a serialized lecture. Shuffle the clips. A tip on Monday, a story on Wednesday, an opinion on Friday. Nobody should be able to reconstruct your recording session from your feed.

Mistake 3: Skipping the hook rewrite. The first line you said in the recording is almost never the best first line for the Short. It's fine — expected, even — to re-record a two-second spoken hook or overlay stronger text on the opening frame. The clip earns its views in the first two seconds; give those two seconds the most attention, not the least.

The math

One 45-minute recording session per week. Roughly 90 minutes of mining and briefing. Editing outsourced or batched. Output: 5–7 Shorts, posted daily, each one a complete idea.

Compare that to the old way — 5–7 separate filming sessions, 5–7 separate edits, and a posting schedule held together by guilt. The repurposing workflow isn't a hack. It's just how the math works out once you stop treating every Short as a special occasion.
