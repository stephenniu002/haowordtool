I started making Shorts the hard way: filming on my phone, cutting clips in a free editor, and spending four hours on a video that got 40 views. It was unsustainable, so I did what any reasonable person would do — I threw AI at the problem.

Over the last few months I've tested most of the AI video approaches people actually use for short-form content. Here's what worked, what didn't, and the honest verdict on each.

1. Text-to-video generators

You type a prompt, the tool generates footage. Tools in this category have gotten dramatically better in a short time — realistic motion, coherent scenes, decent faces most of the time.

The catch: "most of the time" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Hands still melt. Physics still glitches. And every video comes out looking like it was made by the same tool, because it was. For faceless channels and background b-roll, it's fine. For anything where you're the brand, it's a non-starter.

Verdict: Great for b-roll and faceless channels. Weak for personal brands.

2. AI avatar / talking-head tools

These put a digital presenter in front of your script. The use case is real: product explainers, language content, any video where someone needs to talk to camera but you don't want to film yourself.

I've tested these hands-on, and they genuinely work — provided you keep clips short and don't ask the avatar to do anything dramatic. Where people go wrong is expecting one tool to do everything: generate the script, the voice, the avatar, and the edit. Every step you hand to a generalist tool is a step where quality leaks out.

Verdict: Useful for explainers and demos. Don't expect cinematic results from a one-click pipeline.

3. AI-assisted editing (captions, cuts, reframing)

This is the least glamorous category and the one that saved me the most time. Auto-captioning with word-by-word highlights, silence removal, and auto-reframing horizontal footage to vertical — these are solved problems now, and they work well.

I still review every caption file before publishing, because AI mangles names and technical terms. But the 80% it gets right would have taken me hours by hand.

Verdict: The highest ROI of the three. Use it, but proofread.

The approach that actually stuck

Here's what I learned after all that testing: the tools are good at production, but terrible at judgment. The winning workflow isn't "AI does everything" — it's AI handling the mechanical parts while a human makes the decisions that matter: the hook, the pacing, the thumbnail, the title.

My honest recommendation

If you're just starting out: use free AI editing tools for captions and cuts, film yourself for the rest, and publish a lot. Volume teaches you faster than any tool.
